The invention of the transistor is usually dated to December 1947, when
John Bardeen and Walter Brattain, working under William Shockley at Bell
Telephone Laboratories, demonstrated a point-contact device that could
amplify an electrical signal using a piece of germanium. The achievement
was the culmination of decades of work on vacuum tubes, crystal detectors,
and the physics of semiconductors, and it opened a path that the industry
followed for the rest of the century. Within a few years the point-contact
design gave way to the junction transistor, which was easier to manufacture
and far more reliable, and by the middle of the 1950s several companies
were producing transistors in quantity for radios, computers, and military
equipment.

The next great step was integration. In 1958 Jack Kilby at Texas
Instruments built a circuit in which several components were formed on a
single piece of semiconductor material, and shortly afterward Robert Noyce
at Fairchild Semiconductor devised a practical method of connecting those
components with a layer of metal deposited on the surface of the chip.
These two ideas together defined the integrated circuit, and the
manufacturing process that grew out of them made it possible to place
thousands, then millions, then billions of transistors on a single die.
The pace of that growth was noticed early: in 1965 Gordon Moore observed
that the number of components per chip was doubling at a regular interval,
and his observation, later named Moore's law, became both a description of
the industry and a target that the industry organized itself to meet.

Making that progress real required advances in many separate fields. The
growth of pure silicon crystals, the oxidation of silicon surfaces, the
patterning of materials with light-sensitive polymers, the implantation of
dopant ions, and the deposition of thin films of metal and insulator all
had to be understood and controlled to extraordinary precision. Each
generation of technology demanded cleaner rooms, purer chemicals, and more
exact optical systems, and the capital cost of a fabrication plant rose
with every generation. Even so, the economic reward for building smaller
and faster devices was so large that the investment continued for decades
without interruption.

Design methods changed as well. In the early years engineers drew
transistor circuits by hand and verified them with physical models and
breadboards. As the number of devices on a chip grew, that approach became
impossible, and computer programs took over both the simulation of
circuits and the layout of masks. Hardware description languages allowed a
designer to describe behavior rather than geometry, and synthesis tools
converted those descriptions into networks of gates. The result was that a
single engineer could command millions of transistors without ever
touching the details of their physical construction.

The consequences reached far beyond electronics. Cheap computation
transformed communication, medicine, finance, transportation, and
entertainment, and it changed the daily habits of billions of people. It
also created new problems: the energy consumed by large computing systems,
the difficulty of keeping software secure, the concentration of
manufacturing in a small number of regions, and the question of how to
share the benefits of automation fairly. None of these problems has a
simple answer, and each of them is now the subject of serious study in
universities, governments, and industry.

Looking back, the transistor story is a reminder that a single invention
rarely stands alone. It depends on earlier science, on tools that did not
exist when the idea was formed, and on institutions willing to fund work
whose value would not be known for many years. The people who built the
first devices could not have predicted the shape of the industry that
followed, and yet the principles they established, careful measurement,
honest reporting of results, and steady improvement of the process, remain
the foundation of the field today. Students who enter the discipline now
inherit both the achievements and the unsolved questions, and the work
ahead is at least as demanding as the work that came before.
